# Movers vs brokers: what's the difference?

A mover, called a household-goods motor carrier in federal law, transports your belongings, usually with its own trucks and crews, and is responsible for transporting them. A moving broker is paid to arrange your move with a carrier and doesn't transport anything itself. Both are legal and regulated. American Moving Choice works only with movers.

## What is a mover, and what is a moving broker?

[Counsel: definitions worded with counsel (implications §7.1)]

### A mover is a household-goods motor carrier

Federal law calls a company that transports goods by motor vehicle for pay a motor carrier ([49 U.S.C. 13102(14)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title49-section13102&num=0&edition=prelim)). A household-goods motor carrier is a motor carrier that, as a regular part of its household-goods business, offers some or all of these services: binding and non-binding estimates, inventories, packing and unpacking at your home, and loading and unloading at your home (49 U.S.C. 13102(12)).

The definition doesn't cover a carrier when it moves household goods in a container or trailer that is loaded and unloaded entirely by you or someone else who isn't its employee or agent.

To move household goods between states, a mover needs household-goods carrier authority from the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration (FMCSA). A mover that works only within one state follows that state's rules instead and may hold no FMCSA authority.

[Verify: carrier-vs-broker note B2 (search excerpts of 49 U.S.C. 13102(12) and (14); no separate excerpt for 13102(12)(C)); interstate authority and intrastate movers from compliance facts §1 (search excerpts of FMCSA registration pages and Form OP-1) and note B14 (FMCSA's NOT AUTHORIZED FAQ: the status doesn't apply to intrastate-only operations).]

### A moving broker arranges moves that carriers perform

Federal law defines a broker as a company, other than a motor carrier or a carrier's employee or agent, that for pay sells, offers, negotiates or arranges transportation by a motor carrier, or advertises or presents itself as doing so ([49 U.S.C. 13102(2)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title49-section13102&num=0&edition=prelim)). FMCSA's rules use nearly the same definition for moves: a household-goods broker does those things for the transportation of household goods by a motor carrier. Where the law leaves out a carrier's agents, the rules leave out only its bona fide agents ([49 CFR 371.103](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/subtitle-B/chapter-III/subchapter-B/part-371/subpart-B)). FMCSA's application form for operating authority (Form OP-1) describes a household-goods broker as arranging moves through an authorized carrier without taking possession of the goods or responsibility for them.

A mover can arrange its own moves without being a broker, but only moves it's authorized to carry out and has accepted and legally bound itself to carry out. The same applies to the mover's employees and its bona fide agents ([49 CFR 371.2](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/subtitle-B/chapter-III/subchapter-B/part-371/subpart-A/section-371.2)).

[Verify: carrier-vs-broker note B1, B3, B4 and A6 (search excerpts of 49 U.S.C. 13102(2), 49 CFR 371.2 and 371.103, and FMCSA's Form OP-1 instructions).]

### How a carrier's agent differs from a broker

Some movers work as agents of a national van line. The rules treat a carrier's bona fide agent differently from a broker:

- **A carrier's bona fide agent** is part of that carrier's normal organization. It works under the carrier's direction, under a standing agreement, and has no say in giving your move to some other carrier ([49 CFR 371.2](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/subtitle-B/chapter-III/subchapter-B/part-371/subpart-A/section-371.2)).
- **A broker** is paid to arrange transportation by a carrier, and isn't that carrier's bona fide agent (49 CFR 371.2(a), 371.103).

The household-goods rules name two kinds of agent: a prime agent, which provides moving services for its carrier, including selling or arranging moves, and an emergency or temporary agent, which handles only the pickup or delivery end of a move. A household-goods broker isn't a prime agent ([49 CFR 375.205](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/49/375.205)).

A van line is responsible for what its agents do on your move when they act within the authority it gave them or appeared to give them, or when it later approves what they did, and it must take care in choosing them ([49 U.S.C. 13907](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/49/13907)).

## Movers and brokers compared

This table covers moves between states, which FMCSA regulates.

| | Mover (household-goods motor carrier) | Moving broker |
|---|---|---|
| **Who moves your things** | The mover, with its own trucks and crews, or for a van line, those of its agents | A carrier the broker arranges. A household-goods broker may use only a carrier with an active USDOT number and FMCSA authority to move household goods. FMCSA says brokers usually have no trucks, though some registered brokers do |
| **FMCSA authority it holds** | Household-goods carrier authority, as a common or contract carrier | Household-goods broker authority, to arrange moves through an authorized carrier. A broker must register with FMCSA, but FMCSA says moving brokers don't receive an "authorized" designation |
| **Federal financial filings** | Liability insurance (BI&PD) of at least $750,000 for vehicles rated at 10,001 pounds or more. A common carrier also files cargo insurance of $5,000 per vehicle and $10,000 per occurrence, a minimum, not full coverage for your belongings; a contract carrier files no cargo insurance | A $75,000 surety bond or trust fund |
| **Estimates** | A written estimate, binding or non-binding, based on its own survey of your belongings in person or by video, unless you waive the survey in writing before loading. [How movers charge](/moving-costs/how-movers-charge/) explains the rules | Only a written estimate given on behalf of one specific mover: based on that mover's published prices (its tariff) and on that mover's survey of your belongings, in person or by video, unless you waive the survey in writing before loading. That mover must first have agreed in writing to adopt the broker's estimate as its own |
| **Moving day** | The mover's crew, or for a van line, its agent's crew | The crew of the carrier the broker arranged. FMCSA notes that a broker may fail to place your move with a carrier, leaving you without a mover on moving day |
| **Loss or damage claims** | You file a written claim with the mover. ## How can you tell if a company is a broker?

Federal rules require a household-goods broker that arranges moves between states for people moving their own belongings to say what it is. On its website and in its ads, it must show prominently:

- its physical business address;
- its USDOT number and MC number;
- that it's a household-goods broker, that it won't transport your belongings, and that it will arrange for a carrier with FMCSA authority to move them, at charges set by that carrier's published tariff;
- if it gives estimates for a carrier, that each estimate is based on that carrier's tariff, which you can ask to see.

It may show the names or logos only of carriers it has a written agreement with ([49 CFR 371.107](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/subtitle-B/chapter-III/subchapter-B/part-371/subpart-B/section-371.107)). Every broker must advertise only under the name it's registered under, can't present itself as a carrier, and must show in its ads that it's a broker ([49 CFR 371.7](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/subtitle-B/chapter-III/subchapter-B/part-371)).

A household-goods broker must also give you:

- a list of all the carriers it uses, with their USDOT and MC numbers, and a statement that it isn't a motor carrier authorized to move your belongings and only arranges for an authorized carrier to do so ([49 CFR 371.109](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/subtitle-B/chapter-III/subchapter-B/part-371/subpart-B/section-371.109));
- FMCSA's brochure "Ready to Move?" and booklet "Your Rights and Responsibilities When You Move", as links or on paper (49 CFR 371.111);
- its policies on cancellation, deposits and refunds if you cancel before a carrier is due to pick up your belongings. These must also be on its website and in its agreement with you (49 CFR 371.117).

So if a company's website, ads or paperwork say it's a household-goods broker, you're dealing with a broker. If a company tells you it's the mover, check that its FMCSA record shows household-goods carrier authority and no broker authority, as the next section explains.

## How to read a company's FMCSA record

Interstate movers and brokers must register with FMCSA, so each has a public record. Search by USDOT number rather than by name, because different companies can have similar names. FMCSA's [Protect Your Move](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/protect-your-move/how-to/faqs) site lets you search interstate movers and brokers by name, state, USDOT number or MC number and see their complaint history.

### The company snapshot

FMCSA's company snapshot ([SAFER](https://safer.fmcsa.dot.gov/saferhelp.aspx)) shows three fields that matter here:

| Field | What it can say | What to look for in a mover |
|---|---|---|
| Entity type | CARRIER, BROKER, or a combined value | CARRIER, and not BROKER |
| USDOT status | ACTIVE, INACTIVE or OUT-OF-SERVICE | ACTIVE |
| Operating authority status | AUTHORIZED FOR, followed by the kinds of authority held, such as HHG for household goods; or NOT AUTHORIZED | AUTHORIZED FOR, including HHG; then check that broker authority reads None on Licensing and Insurance |

Being registered with FMCSA isn't the same as being authorized to move household goods: a company can be registered without that authority ([FMCSA's registered-versus-authorized FAQ](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/consumer-protection/protect-your-move/are-fmcsa-registered-moving-companies-same-fmcsa-authorized)). NOT AUTHORIZED means the company has no operating authority, or isn't authorized for interstate work for pay. FMCSA says that status doesn't apply to companies that operate only within one state ([FMCSA's NOT AUTHORIZED FAQ](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/faq/why-my-operating-authority-status-shown-not-authorized-safety-and-fitness-electronic-records)).

The snapshot may not make broker authority clear. Check the Broker row on Licensing and Insurance, below: for a mover, it should read None.

ACTIVE tells you a little less than it used to. While FMCSA moves registration to its new Motus system, it has paused marking USDOT numbers inactive when a company misses the registration update required every two years, for updates due on or after June 1, 2026 ([FMCSA's biennial update notice](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/registration/temporary-suspension-biennial-update-requirement)).

### Why an MC number alone doesn't tell you

An MC number is a docket number tied to a company's operating authority. The number alone doesn't show what kind of authority the company holds: its record lists carrier and broker authority separately, and a company can hold both. A company that gets new authority through Motus receives a separate docket number for each authority, so it can have more than one. Check the authority types, not just the number.

Since FMCSA launched Motus in May 2026, new USDOT and docket numbers are assigned at random, and existing numbers haven't changed. So how a number looks, such as its length or first digits, tells you nothing about the company. Motus also gives new registrations letter suffixes, such as B for broker authority. FMCSA says companies registering for the first time see them in their Motus account, and existing USDOT numbers don't have them, so don't rely on a suffix to spot a broker.

## Questions to ask any moving company

Ask these before you sign anything or pay a deposit.

- **Are you the company that will move my belongings, with your own trucks and crews?** FMCSA suggests asking this. If the answer is no, ask which company will.
- **What's your USDOT number, and your MC number?** Look the company up yourself, as described above, and check that the name on the record matches the company you're talking to.
- **Whose name will be on my estimate and my bill of lading?** The bill of lading is the contract and receipt for your move. The name on it and on the estimate tells you which carrier takes responsibility for your belongings. A broker can give you an estimate only on behalf of a specific carrier that has agreed to it in writing.

If the company is a broker, also ask:

- **Which carriers do you use?** It must give you a list with their USDOT and MC numbers. Check those carriers on FMCSA's records too.
- **Do you have a written agreement with the carrier?** FMCSA suggests asking.
- **Is the estimate in writing, and based on the carrier's tariff?** FMCSA advises getting a written estimate based on the actual mover's tariff, never a verbal one.
- **What are your cancellation, deposit and refund policies?** A broker must publish them on its website and put them in its agreement with you.

## Do state rules apply too?

Yes. Moves within one state follow that state's rules, and a state can regulate moving brokers as well as movers. Florida, for example, requires movers and moving brokers to register with its [Department of Agriculture and Consumer Services](https://www.fdacs.gov/Business-Services/Moving-Companies) (FDACS), and a Florida moving broker may arrange moves only with registered movers ([Florida Statutes, chapter 507](https://www.leg.state.fl.us/statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0500-0599%2F0507%2F0507.html)). Other states have their own rules, which this page doesn't cover.
